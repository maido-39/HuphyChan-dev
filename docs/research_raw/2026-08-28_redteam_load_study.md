## 1. Sim-to-real load gap — direction and magnitude

**What external literature says:**
- **Validating Robotics Simulators on Real-World Impacts** (Acosta et al., [arXiv:2110.00541](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00541)) is the most directly relevant paper found: it benchmarks Drake, MuJoCo, and Bullet against real impact data on two cases — a tossed cube and a **Cassie biped landing from a jump**. Headline finding: *simulators capture inelastic impacts well but fail to capture elastic impacts*; the paper attributes most of the simulator-to-simulator and simulator-to-real spread to "system-specific contact parameters" and identifies contact/friction modeling choices as "the largest distinctions between popular tools." No single clean percent-error number was extractable from the abstract-level fetch (would need the full PDF/table for exact figures) — **flag as a follow-up if this becomes a citable design number**, but the qualitative conclusion (compliant/high-damping contact underestimates elastic rebound, i.e. is more forgiving on rebound-driven force spikes than reality) is a documented, peer-reviewed result, not our conjecture.
- **PACE** (leggedrobotics, [github](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/pace-sim2real)) and **S3N — Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap in Parallel-Link Leg Mechanisms** ([arXiv:2608.01697](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.01697)) both exist specifically because uncorrected sim GRF is wrong enough on real parallel-link/leg hardware to need a dedicated dynamics-normalization step: S3N reports the normalization step *cuts* GRF-norm RMSE by 65.1%/62.4% in two motions, which implies the **uncorrected baseline error was large enough that a majority of it is removable by better actuator/joint dynamics identification alone** — i.e., a big chunk of "sim-to-real load gap" is explainable by uncalibrated actuator dynamics, not unknowable physics.
- **Sim-to-Real of Humanoid Locomotion Policies via Joint Torque Space Perturbation Injection** ([arXiv:2504.06585](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.06585)) proposes injecting perturbations directly in torque space (as an alternative/supplement to parameter-space domain randomization) specifically because standard DR does not cover the actual torque-tracking reality gap on humanoid hardware. Full quantitative tables were not extractable via automated fetch (page-limit/parsing issue) — **treat the existence and framing of this paper as evidence that torque-space (not just parameter-space) gaps are a live, unsolved problem in 2025 humanoid RL**, not as a source of a specific number.
- **Decoupling Reaction Force Control and Gait Control** ([arXiv:2509.13737](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.13737)) argues that coupling GRF control and gait/position control inside a single RL policy (the standard recipe) *amplifies* sim-to-real problems relative to decoupled architectures — a structural argument that our single-policy, position-tracking-reward architecture is, by construction, more sim-to-real-fragile on the force axis than it looks on the tracking-error axis.
- **Actuator-net paradigm** (Hwangbo et al. 2019, ANYmal, [arXiv:1901.08652](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.08652)): the entire reason actuator nets exist is that analytic motor models (what we largely use, modulo the RobStride measured T-N curves) are known to mismatch real torque delivery under load — the standard fix is a learned actuator model trained on logged hardware current/torque data, which this project has not done (we use measured static T-N envelopes + measured no-load speed, not a dynamic actuator-net).

**Our own documented sim-to-real gaps (internal, already measured):**
- RobStride RS04 no-load speed: **sim 33 rad/s vs real 19.9 rad/s** (measured T-N curve at 48V) — a ~40% gap on a single, easily-measured static parameter (memory: `reference-robstride-motor-specs`). If a parameter this simple to characterize is 40% off before any RL training even starts, that sets a plausible *lower bound* on how far to distrust anything the policy does with dynamic torque/GRF that has never been hardware-validated at all.
- **Unitree G1 real rosbag cross-check** (`docs/65_design_value_uncertainty.md` §9): after mass/leg-length scaling (k=1.78) and restricting to a matched low-speed regime, our sim joint torque RMS/P99 land within **0.5–2.1×** of real G1 hardware (geometric mean ratio RMS 0.84, P99 0.79 — i.e. sim is *slightly conservative/low*, not inflated). This is the single strongest piece of evidence this project has that its *absolute scale* is not fantastical — but it is one cross-check, one robot, one gait style (straight-leg G1 vs our bent-knee crouch), and the note itself flags per-joint deviations (knee 2.1× high, hip_roll 0.5× low, ankle_roll 0.35× low) as gait-style-driven, not physics-driven, i.e. **it validates order-of-magnitude, not per-joint design values**.
- Solver/engine cross-check (`docs/research_raw/2026-08-27_physx_mujoco_contact_matching.md`): PhysX default `max_depenetration_velocity` (100 m/s) vs. what every official IsaacLab biped/quadruped config actually ships (1.0 m/s) is a **100× difference in how hard the engine "kicks" a barely-penetrating foot back out** — an engine-default footgun that would silently inflate cross-engine GRF comparisons if not caught. This was caught, but it is exactly the kind of invisible-until-audited parameter a red-team review must explicitly check for on both sides of any cross-engine comparison.

**Net read:** the literature does not give one clean "sim underestimates GRF by X%" number to plug in. It gives (a) a peer-reviewed finding that compliant contact models (MuJoCo-like) are *inelastic-biased* — plausibly conservative on rebound/elastic spikes, and (b) a consistent story across three independent papers that most of the *dynamic* torque/GRF gap traces to actuator-dynamics miscalibration and engine defaults, which are both checkable and (partially) already checked in this project, rather than an irreducible unknown.

## 2. Reward-hacking detection — external corroboration and invariants

**Formal framing:**
- Amodei et al., **Concrete Problems in AI Safety** ([arXiv:1606.06565](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.06565)) — the canonical taxonomy (reward hacking via partially-observed goals, Goodhart's-law metric collapse under optimization pressure, self-reinforcing feedback loops, wireheading) that everything downstream cites. Their own robotics example (a cleaning robot rewarded for bleach consumption pours bleach down the drain) is structurally identical to our v9 "uncapped work reward → GRF 11.5×BW" incident: an unconstrained proxy for a good behavior (cleanliness / powerful push-off) is optimized directly, and the correlation with the real goal (clean floor / low-impact push-off) breaks under optimization pressure.
- Skalse et al., **Defining and Characterizing Reward Hacking** ([arXiv:2209.13085](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.13085), NeurIPS 2022) gives the formal condition: hacking occurs when a proxy reward is *not policy-independent* of the true objective, i.e. optimizing the proxy past some point *decreases* true-objective value. This gives a testable definition for our GRF case: if pushing `ankle_pushoff_work` harder increases measured GRF/impact beyond the physically-necessary push-off, that is the formal signature, not just "looks unphysical."
- Emergent-Mind survey on **legged-robot-adjacent reward hacking** describes an Ant-v5 augmentation where a policy exploits a sensor-saturation loophole (reward bonus scales with contact force once above a threshold) to *deliberately* produce oversized GRF — a close external analogue of "a reward term that scales with force/impact, without an upper cap, gets exploited to inflate force," independently confirming the mechanism behind our own v9 incident (`docs/62` Principle 5/6) rather than it being an idiosyncrasy of our reward stack.
- A **comprehensive empirical detection study** (Iowa State thesis/repo) reports 78.4% precision / 81.7% recall for automated reward-hacking detection across 15,247 episodes on Atari/MuJoCo-gym benchmarks. This is **not legged-hardware-load specific** and the task distribution (game/gym benchmarks) is far from ours — cite only as evidence that *generic automated reward-hacking detectors exist and are imperfect (≈80%)*, not as a tool we could import directly.

**Invariants (what should hold if a load number is physically real, not an artifact):**
- **Vertical impulse ≈ mg·T** over a full gait cycle (steady-state, constant CoM velocity): confirmed as a standard biomechanics sanity check — "the time-averaged vertical GRF over a gait cycle must equal body weight" (impulse-momentum theorem applied to gait, corroborated by multiple gait-analysis sources found in search, e.g. general-relationship-links-gait-mechanics literature). **Actionable**: any measured GRF trace whose time-average over full strides deviates materially from 1.0×BW (net vertical) is either not steady-state (accelerating/decelerating — check command box coverage) or is an artifact (contact-detection bug, missing foot channel, resampling error) — this is a nearly-free check we are not documented as running.
- **AP impulse balance**: in steady-state level gait, braking impulse ≈ propulsion impulse (net AP impulse ≈ 0). A policy whose measured AP GRF integral is systematically nonzero at "constant-velocity" commands is either not actually at steady state (still accelerating within the dwell window — a soft flag for our own 15s-dwell protocol, since 15s may still contain visible accel/decel transients at command transitions) or has an unbalanced fore-aft gait asymmetry that the reward missed.
- **CoP inside the support polygon** whenever GRF_z > 0 (rigid-body contact validity condition) — general contact-mechanics/support-polygon result (Wikipedia support-polygon page, static-CoP-sensitivity literature). A measured CoP outside the actual foot geometry during a nonzero-force contact indicates a geometry/contact-model bug (e.g. numerical CoP computed from a wrench that includes non-foot contact, or foot geometry mismatch between XML and post-hoc CoP script) rather than a real physical event, since it's a physical impossibility for a rigid planar foot.
- **Force-direction / friction-cone validity**: contact tangential force must stay inside μ·N (friction cone). A measured GRF sample that implies an effective friction coefficient above what's configured in the contact solver (our friction=1.0) is a solver/measurement bug, not a real load — cheap to check per-sample and directly catches some classes of solver-instability artifacts.
- **Power balance / no free lunch**: net mechanical power into the system from actuators + gravity should track CoM kinetic+potential energy change to within damping/friction losses — a coarse but useful check against the "unbounded work reward → free energy" class of hacks (this project's own v9), independent of whether GRF itself looks plausible.

**How this maps onto our v9/soft-landing history**: our own catalogue (`docs/62` failure list; `docs/research_raw/2026-08-24_soft_landing_research.md` §3 hacking-mode table) is, on external comparison, not an unusual set of failure modes — every one of "uncapped power/work reward exploited," "absolute-threshold creep-gaming," "static-curl gaming of a direct torque penalty," and "one dense new term drowned by a sparse old one" has a named external analogue (Hwangbo curriculum staging for effort penalties; van Marum single-stance-contact fix for double-hopping; the Skalse formal hacking definition for the curl case). This is *reassuring for methodology* (we're catching the known failure classes) but **not evidence the current v2s1 policy is clean** — it's evidence the failure taxonomy is complete enough to check against, not that the checking has been done for v2s1 specifically.

## 3. Single-seed / single-policy sizing risk

- **Colas, Sigaud & Oudeyer, How Many Random Seeds? Statistical Power Analysis in Deep RL Experiments** ([arXiv:1806.08295](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.08295)) is the standard citation: it shows that typical deep-RL papers under-report seed counts relative to what's needed for adequate statistical power, and provides a formal method (power analysis via t-test / bootstrap CI) to compute how many seeds a given claimed effect size needs — the answer is usually **more than the 1-3 seeds common in RL papers**, and scales with how large a difference you're trying to detect reliably.
- Corroborating: **Islam et al., Reproducibility of Benchmarked Deep RL Tasks for Continuous Control** ([arXiv:1708.04133](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.04133)) and the broader "reproducibility crisis" literature (Semmelrock et al. 2025, AI Magazine) document that two training runs of the *same algorithm* differing only in seed can produce statistically significantly different learning curves/behaviors — directly relevant because our design values (docs/65) are currently anchored to **one policy per terrain** (`gen21p2_fc` for flat, `p2b_v2_fc` for rough), with seed-level variance explicitly listed as an open gap (docs/65 §7: "멀티시드 3~5로 정책간 분산 추가 → 총 불확실성" — not yet done) and docs/62 §3b flags the same gap.
- **What a credible study basis looks like**: the general-purpose-RL-benchmarking convention (Colas et al., Henderson-adjacent reproducibility work) is to report **aggregate statistics (mean ± CI) across ≥5-10 seeds** for any claim used to *compare* configurations, with the number driven by a power calculation against the effect size that matters (here: does knee P99 shift by more than the ~7% CI width already measured intra-seed via block-bootstrap in docs/65 §1?). Our intra-seed CI (block-bootstrap, ±4-18% for P99) answers "how noisy is this one training run's measurement," it does **not** answer "how much would a different seed of the same reward/curriculum, or a different but equally-valid reward tweak, move the number" — those are different variance sources and the literature is explicit that they don't collapse into one number.
- **Worst-case-over-seeds vs. distribution**: no literature source found gives a clean answer specific to hardware load sizing (this looks like a gap in the field, not just in our search) — but the general engineering-statistics answer (extreme-value theory, §6 below) argues for **treating seed-to-seed policy variation as an additional random variable to be convolved into the same P99/return-level framework**, not for picking either "max over seeds" (too sensitive to one bad/degenerate seed, per our own peak-provenance findings in docs/63) or "single seed" (current state) as the basis.

**Status for our project**: this is a documented, acknowledged, *not yet closed* gap (docs/65 §7, docs/62 §3b both list "멀티시드 3-5" as a to-do item), and external literature independently confirms it is not a minor concern — it's flagged as a first-order threat to any *comparative* claim (including "v2s1 is better/worse than gen21p2") and a real, if harder to quantify externally, threat to absolute design values.

## 4. Domain-randomization envelope vs. real-world distribution

- **Understanding Domain Randomization for Sim-to-real Transfer** ([arXiv:2110.03239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.03239)) gives the formal framing: DR trains a policy that's optimal for the *expected* value across a distribution of MDPs; sim-to-real gap bounds are derived under the assumption that the true real-world parameter lies *within* the randomized set. **If it does not**, none of the theoretical guarantees apply — the bound is conditional on coverage, not unconditional.
- Multiple sources (BayRnTune, DROPO, general DR surveys) converge on the same two-sided failure mode: **too-narrow DR** biases the policy toward a fixed operating point that breaks outside it (this project's own OOD finding: blind-rough velocities 490-953 rpm are "인공물" because they're outside the trained command box, docs/62 §3a point 2); **too-wide DR** produces an *overly conservative* policy whose in-distribution behavior is degraded (a distinct risk from what we've been measuring — we've mostly worried about narrow DR under-covering, not wide DR making the "worst case" itself softer/more conservative than a narrower, more targeted DR would show).
- **No source found that gives a formula for "how much margin beyond the measured real-world envelope should DR carry to be called conservative."** This appears to be an open/practice-dependent question industry-wide, not something we're missing relative to a known standard. The practical answer in the literature is empirical-iterative: widen DR, retrain, re-measure, check if the "worst case" migrates to the new boundary (indicating the boundary, not real physics, is still driving the number) or stabilizes inside the range (our own documented practice, docs/62 §3a point 2: "학습 DR을 넓혀 최악 스케줄을 in-DR로 만들기" — this is exactly the right iterative test, already adopted).
- **What would make the argument "our DR range is conservative" strong** (synthesized from the above, not a direct citation): (a) show the in-DR worst-case metric is NOT sitting at the DR boundary (i.e., not a monotonically-increasing function of DR range that hasn't saturated) — docs/65's own peak-provenance work (docs/63) already does a version of this for *command* ranges; it is not documented as having been done for the *physical* DR parameters (mass, friction, motor gain noise, etc.) the same way; (b) cross-reference DR bounds against measured real-world variation of the same physical parameters (e.g., real motor-to-motor torque constant spread, real friction coefficient range for the actual foot material) rather than picking DR ranges by convention/prior-project inheritance; (c) explicitly distinguish "DR added for training robustness" (may be deliberately wide, e.g. push kicks) from "DR meant to bound a design load" (should be calibrated to real physical uncertainty, not robustness-training convenience) — these can silently be the same numbers in configs without anyone checking whether that's appropriate for both uses.

## 5. Measurement-side artifacts

- **200 Hz physics / 50 Hz control sampling gap** (this project's own finding, `docs/research_raw/2026-08-24_soft_landing_research.md` §6 point 5): true peak GRF (200 Hz, e.g. AB max 2.17 BW) is materially higher than the 50 Hz-subsampled P99 (1.41 BW) — this is flagged internally as "a structural blind spot any reward term faces," not a bug, and is exactly the kind of thing SAE J211 (crash-test instrumentation standard, [law.resource.org](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/cfr/ibr/005/sae.j211-1.1995.html)) formalizes for automotive impact testing via **Channel Frequency Class (CFC)**: the standard mandates a minimum sampling rate (~10 kHz for its highest-fidelity CFC-1000 channels) specifically so that peak values are not lost to aliasing/decimation, and explicitly ties required sampling rate to the frequency content of the phenomenon being measured, not to a control-loop-convenient rate. **This is directly transferable framing**: a red-teamer with an automotive/impact-testing background will ask "what's your channel frequency class equivalent, and why is your peak-measurement rate (200 Hz physics, sometimes 50 Hz reward-visible) tied to your control loop instead of to the contact-event bandwidth (foot-ground impact rise time)?" — our own note already does the right diagnostic (compare 200 Hz vs 50 Hz explicitly) but has not benchmarked against an external "how fast is fast enough" standard.
- **Solver/engine contact-stiffness divergence**: `docs/research_raw/2026-08-27_physx_mujoco_contact_matching.md` and `2026-08-27_physx_closed_loop_ankle.md` (both already produced this week) document that MuJoCo's compliant (spring-damper, `solref`/`solimp`) contact and PhysX's rigid-impulse contact are **not the same physical model**, and that at least one major, easy-to-miss config default (`max_depenetration_velocity` 100 vs 1.0 m/s) can single-handedly cause an order-of-magnitude-scale artifact in cross-engine GRF comparison. External corroboration: SimBenchmark (ETH Zurich, [leggedrobotics.github.io/SimBenchmark](https://leggedrobotics.github.io/SimBenchmark/)) explicitly classifies MuJoCo as "soft contact" vs. RaiSim/Bullet/ODE/DART as "hard contact" and states MuJoCo's soft model "cannot control elasticity of contact" — i.e. the two families are not expected to agree on peak/elastic-rebound-dominated metrics (GRF peak) even with "correct" configuration, only on quantities less sensitive to short-timescale contact stiffness (impulse, duty cycle, timing) — which is *exactly* the agree/disagree split already observed in our own cross-check. This is a second, independent (peer-reviewed-adjacent) confirmation that our cross-engine protocol's finding ("agree on impulse/duty/timing, NOT on peak GRF unless solver iterations stated") is the expected/correct behavior of these two engine families, not a bug in our port.
- **Contact/solver iteration count and convergence**: general contact-solver literature (arXiv:2405.17020, "compliant to rigid contact") confirms iterative contact solvers trade peak-force accuracy for compute — under-converged solves systematically **understate peak contact force** (the solver hasn't pushed penetration to zero yet when it stops iterating), which is a distinct failure mode from the sampling-rate aliasing above (both under-report peaks, but for different mechanistic reasons — solver non-convergence vs. temporal decimation — so fixing one does not fix the other, and both need separate audit line items).
- **Practical checklist implied**: (1) state and log solver iteration count / convergence tolerance whenever a peak/P99 GRF number is reported, especially cross-engine; (2) report peak/P99 at the *physics* rate (200 Hz) as the honest number, and separately report the reward-visible/50 Hz-decimated number, labeled as such, never conflate the two; (3) treat any single-engine "peak" as solver-parameter-dependent unless a convergence sweep (varying iteration count / timestep) has been shown to be flat.

## 6. The safety-factor stack — is RMS×1.15 / P99×1.25 defensible?

- **No engineering standard maps directly onto "RMS×1.15 for thermal, P99×1.25 for instantaneous."** The closest formal analogues found:
  - **Motor thermal derating** (vendor practice, e.g. OpenELAB's humanoid actuator selection guide): the standard approach is to use vendor-supplied derating curves against measured RMS torque relative to continuous rating, plus a **dedicated long-duration thermal soak test** on a representative mounted link (not just a computed number) — i.e. industry does use an RMS-based continuous-rating check structurally similar to ours, but backs it with **physical thermal validation**, not a bare ×1.15 multiplier alone. Our RMS×1.15 lacks the soak-test half of the standard practice.
  - **Bearing L10 life** (NHBB, powertransmission.com): a formal fatigue-life statistic (90% survival at rated life, exponent 3.0 roller / 3.33 ball) computed from a **load spectrum integrated over the full duty cycle with a defined service factor per application class**, not a percentile of measured torque. A red-teamer from a bearing-design background would ask for an **L10 calculation from our RMS/duty data**, not just an RMS comparison to rated torque — this is a materially different (and currently absent) calculation this project has not attempted for its output bearings, despite `docs/56` already flagging output-bearing tilting-moment margin as tight (hip_yaw 145 N·m vs. SHD-20 rated 93 N·m).
  - **AGMA gear service factors**: service factor selection is driven by **duty class tables** (uniform/moderate-shock/heavy-shock load per application), and industrial vs. commercial gearbox L10 targets differ by ~20× (5,000 h vs. 100,000 h) — i.e., "what safety factor is enough" is *itself* a function of a documented duty classification, not a single universal multiplier. Search result explicitly warns against **double-counting**: "load factors inflate loads; factors of safety reduce allowable strength... don't double-count them unless the code explicitly requires it." **This is a direct, specific risk for our stack**: P99×1.25 (a load-side inflation) combined with any additional material/structural factor of safety applied downstream in FEA (docs/62 §4 mentions "구조 정적강도 SF 1.5-2.0" applied to the wrench vector) is exactly the double-counting pattern the mechanical-engineering literature warns about, **unless the two factors are explicitly documented as covering non-overlapping uncertainty sources** (one for load-statistics uncertainty, one for material/manufacturing uncertainty) — this project's docs do not currently make that non-overlap argument explicit.
  - **Extreme-value / return-period methods** (wind/structural engineering: Gumbel/GEV fitting, peaks-over-threshold, gust factors): the mature engineering answer to "what percentile/multiplier should I design to" is **not** an ad hoc percentile×constant but a **fitted extreme-value distribution with an explicit return period** (e.g., 50-year wind load), from which a design value is read off with a stated exceedance probability. A red-teamer from a structural/wind-engineering background will ask: *why P99×1.25 and not a GEV/POT fit to the full campaign's peak distribution, with a stated design exceedance probability (e.g., 1-in-10,000 gait cycles)?* This is a legitimate, higher-rigor alternative that our current block-bootstrap-CI approach (docs/65 §1) is adjacent to but not equivalent to — the bootstrap CI quantifies *sampling uncertainty of the P99 estimate itself*, it does not model the *tail distribution shape* the way GEV/POT would, so it cannot answer "what's the 1-in-N-cycles load" the way a formal extreme-value fit could.
- **Our stack's actual defensible parts**: the CI-based hierarchy in docs/65 §1 (RMS/P99 tight CI, peak wide CI → don't size on peak) is methodologically sound and matches the general engineering instinct that *higher moments/extremes of a finite sample are less reliable estimators* — this is not in dispute in the literature, it is just not usually formalized via bootstrap CI in mechanical design (more commonly via GEV/POT or vendor safety margins). The **novel-but-reasonable** part of our approach (bootstrapping CI on RMS/P99 from single-policy time series) is defensible as a *measurement-uncertainty* quantification; it does not by itself substitute for the *hazard-rate*/return-period question a bearing or gear engineer would actually want answered for a fatigue-life design.

## Caveats on this research pass

1. Two of the most directly relevant arXiv papers (2504.06585 torque-space perturbation injection; 2608.01697 S3N GRF normalization) could not be fully read via automated WebFetch (page-size/parsing limits) — only abstract-level framing was recovered. If these become load-bearing citations for the writeup, they need a direct read (PDF download + manual extraction) rather than reliance on this note's summary.
2. No source was found that gives a field-standard answer to "how much DR margin beyond measured real variation counts as conservative" — this looks like a genuine open question industry-wide (confirmed absent, not just missed in search), so §4 of the checklist should be argued from first principles / project-specific evidence, not from an external standard.
3. The reward-hacking automated-detection precision/recall figures (78.4%/81.7%) come from Atari/MuJoCo-gym benchmarks, not legged-hardware-load studies — cited only to establish that generic detectors are imperfect, not as a transferable tool or number for this project.
4. The Acosta et al. (2110.00541) elastic-vs-inelastic finding is the single most load-bearing external citation for the sim-to-real section and section 5, but this note only captured it at abstract-fidelity; the full paper likely has per-scenario error magnitudes that would strengthen or qualify the "MuJoCo is inelastic/conservative-biased" claim and should be pulled before citing a specific error percentage.
5. This note reasons from the project's own already-written internal docs (docs/56/62/63/65, research_raw physx notes, soft-landing research) as ground truth for "what we've already found" — it does not re-verify those internal claims against raw data, only reconciles them against external literature.
